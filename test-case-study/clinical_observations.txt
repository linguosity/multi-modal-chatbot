SELF-CORRECTION & LEARNING BEHAVIORS
================================================================================

SPONTANEOUS SELF-CORRECTION:
During testing, Sofia spontaneously self-corrected her speech errors on 2
occasions:

Instance 1: While naming a picture (GFTA-3), Sofia said /wæbɪt/ (wabbit - rabbit
with gliding substitution). She paused, appeared to think, then said /ɹæbɪt/
(rabbit - more accurate production with /r/). Clinician did not provide explicit
correction; Sofia's self-correction appeared spontaneous.

Instance 2: During informal play, Sofia said "me go" then immediately said "I go"
(correcting from object pronoun to subject pronoun). This occurred without
clinician prompting, suggesting Sofia has awareness that this form is incorrect.

Clinical interpretation: Sofia's spontaneous self-corrections suggest:
- Emerging phonological and grammatical awareness
- Ability to modify speech when cued by internal feedback
- Potential responsiveness to intervention

RESPONSE TO VISUAL/TACTILE CUES:
Sofia showed improved performance when provided with visual cues:

Example 1: During articulation testing, when Sofia could see the clinician's
mouth (visual model of articulation), her productions improved. For instance,
/r/ productions improved from 100% gliding errors to occasional correct
productions when she could see the clinician's mouth position.

Example 2: During following directions task, when the clinician pointed to or
gestured toward the referenced object/location, Sofia's comprehension and
compliance improved significantly.

Example 3: During picture selection tasks (PPVT-5, EVT-3), Sofia performed better
when she could see the pictures clearly and had adequate time to process.

Clinical interpretation: Sofia has strong visual processing skills and responds
well to visual supports. This is an important strength to leverage in
intervention and classroom teaching.

RESPONSE TO VERBAL-ONLY INSTRUCTIONS:
In contrast to her strong response to visual cues, Sofia's performance decreased
when instructions were verbal-only with no visual support:

Example: When asked to "point to the picture that shows the baby eating," Sofia
looked confused until the clinician pointed to or gestured toward the picture or
the action (eating gesture). Verbal instruction alone was less effective.

This pattern is consistent with her receptive language delays noted on formal
testing and with teacher observations that Sofia "requires visual supports."

INTELLIGIBILITY OBSERVATIONS
================================================================================

CONVERSATIONAL SPEECH INTELLIGIBILITY:

Informal assessment (clinician familiar with child, familiar context):
- Intelligibility: Approximately 65%
- Meaning easily understood with context/familiar referents
- Most errors were phonological (articulation/sound errors)
- Grammatical errors sometimes made meaning ambiguous

Example: "me wan dat" = clear meaning in context ("I want that")
Example: "her is big" = meaning understood but pronoun reference unclear

SINGLE-WORD INTELLIGIBILITY:
- Intelligibility on isolated words (GFTA-3): Approximately 50-60%
- Many words required semantic context for intelligibility
- Highly dependent on familiarity of word
- Consonant errors particularly affecting intelligibility

Example: Sofia said /sɑ/ for "car" in isolation. Meaning unclear without context.
In context of car toys, meaning more obvious.

INTELLIGIBILITY WITH UNFAMILIAR LISTENER:
Though not formally assessed with a naïve listener, based on speech sample and
articulation testing, estimated intelligibility with unfamiliar listener would be
approximately 40-50%.

Evidence: Teacher report that peers frequently ask "what?" and don't understand
Sofia. Mother reports that neighbors need her to translate. Clinician (with some
training in understanding disordered speech) achieved 65% intelligibility only in
context.

FACTORS AFFECTING INTELLIGIBILITY:

Factors improving intelligibility:
- Familiar context
- Familiar communication partner
- Visual supports / observation of referent
- Slower speech rate
- Repetition
- One-on-one setting

Factors decreasing intelligibility:
- Unfamiliar context
- Unfamiliar listener
- Rapid speech rate
- Background noise
- Group/noisy settings
- Abstract or non-visual referents

CLINICAL INTERPRETATION:
Sofia's intelligibility is significantly reduced and represents one of the most
concerning aspects of her speech-language disorder. At age 4;4, children should
have 95-100% intelligibility to unfamiliar listeners. Sofia's estimated 40-50%
intelligibility to unfamiliar listeners is significantly below expectation and is
likely contributing to her social difficulties and peer misunderstandings.

LANGUAGE & CODE-SWITCHING OBSERVATIONS
================================================================================

BILINGUAL/CODE-SWITCHING BEHAVIORS:

During the evaluation, Sofia produced several utterances mixing Spanish and
English or using Spanish forms:

Instance 1 (approximately 30 minutes into evaluation):
When the clinician asked Sofia to describe what the baby doll was doing, Sofia
said "mira" (Spanish for "look" or "watch") while pointing at the doll. She then
followed with "baby sleep" (English).

Interpretation: Sofia switched to Spanish when her English vocabulary/expression
was insufficient. Code-switching to first language when English resources are
limited is a normal bilingual behavior, particularly in children with language
delays.

Instance 2 (approximately 45 minutes into evaluation):
During the PPVT-5, when shown a picture of water, Sofia first said "agua" (Spanish
for "water") then appeared to search for the English word and finally pointed to
confirm understanding. No response needed on test (picture identification, not
naming).

Interpretation: Sofia's Spanish vocabulary is accessible and she may rely on
Spanish when English word-finding is difficult. This could indicate that her
Spanish vocabulary may be more robust than her English vocabulary, which is
expected given her Spanish dominance at home (75% Spanish vs. 25% English).

Instance 3 (approximately 60 minutes into evaluation):
During informal play, when Sofia could not produce an answer to a question
("Can you make the car go fast?"), she said "no sé" (Spanish for "I don't know")
with a gesture.

Interpretation: Code-switching to Spanish to express negation or uncertainty
(formulaic response) may be easier than formulating English response.

CLINICAL INTERPRETATION:

Sofia's code-switching behavior is consistent with:
1. Bilingual language development
2. Language delay in both languages (not just English)
3. Possible Spanish dominance maintained from home environment
4. Emerging bilingual pragmatic skill (using appropriate language based on
   context/partner)

Assessment consideration: Sofia's language delay appears to be present in both
Spanish and English, not limited to English. Her vocabulary and grammatical
skills are delayed in Spanish as well, according to parent report ("she doesn't
combine words as much as her siblings did at this age"). This supports diagnosis
of language disorder rather than simple English language learning issue.

Recommendation: If intervention is pursued, determining the language of service
(Spanish, English, or both) would be important. Family preference and community
needs should guide this decision.
